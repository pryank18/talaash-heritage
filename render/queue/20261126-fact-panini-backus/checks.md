## Caption

In 1967 a computing journal printed a proposal to name a programming notation after Pāṇini, the Sanskrit grammarian.

His Aṣṭādhyāyī sums up Sanskrit phonetics and grammar in about 4,000 sūtras, set out in eight chapters. It became the standard for Classical Sanskrit.

Centuries later, computer scientists wrote the rules of programming languages in a notation called Backus Normal Form or Backus–Naur Form. In 1964 Donald Knuth wrote to Communications of the ACM about those two names.

In March 1967 Peter Zilahy Ingerman of the Radio Corporation of America added a third option in the same journal. His letter's title: "'Pānini-Backus Form' suggested" (vol. 10, no. 3, p. 137). The name never replaced BNF.

In March 1967, Communications of the ACM carried a short letter from Peter Zilahy Ingerman of the Radio Corporation of America titled "'Pānini-Backus Form' suggested" (vol. 10, no. 3, p. 137).

The context: computer scientists described programming-language syntax with a notation known as Backus Normal Form or Backus–Naur Form, and Donald Knuth had written in the same journal in 1964 about which of those names to use. Ingerman proposed crediting Pāṇini, whose Aṣṭādhyāyī sets out Sanskrit phonetics and grammar in about 4,000 sūtras across eight chapters.

The name never replaced BNF, but the letter is a useful footnote where linguistics and computing meet. More on India's language traditions is on the Talaash Heritage website.

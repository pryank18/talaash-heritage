## Caption

"Jungle" comes from Sanskrit jāṅgala, a word for dry land with few trees, not thick forest.

Monier-Williams' Sanskrit-English Dictionary glosses jāṅgala as "arid, sparingly grown with trees and plants". Turner's Comparative Dictionary of the Indo-Aryan Languages gives jaṅgala as "desert region", and lists Pali jaṅgala as "rough waterless place".

Hobson-Jobson, the old dictionary of Anglo-Indian words, explains that the word meant "in strictness only waste, uncultivated ground", then land overgrown with shrubs, trees or long grass. From there it came to mean forest.

English picked it up in India. Merriam-Webster dates its first known use to 1776, and Hobson-Jobson quotes a 1776 law digest: "Land waste for five years ... is called Jungle."

## LinkedIn text

Saturday Word: jungle.

English borrowed "jungle" from Hindi-Urdu jangal, which goes back to Sanskrit jāṅgala. The surprise is in the older meaning. Monier-Williams' Sanskrit-English Dictionary glosses jāṅgala as "arid, sparingly grown with trees and plants", and Turner's Comparative Dictionary of the Indo-Aryan Languages records jaṅgala as "desert region".

Hobson-Jobson, the classic glossary of Anglo-Indian words, explains that the word meant waste, uncultivated ground before it came to mean land covered with shrubs, trees or long grass, and finally forest. Merriam-Webster dates the first known English use to 1776.

A word for dry, open country ended up meaning dense, tangled tropical vegetation. More on India's language history is on the Talaash Heritage website.

### Slide 1.3 (text): Ashoka, the third Maurya emperor
- On screen: Ruled about 268 to 232 BCE
- On screen: Edicts on rocks and pillars, Afghanistan to Karnataka
> Ashoka was the third Maurya emperor. He ruled from about 268 to 232 BCE.
> His edicts, cut into rocks and pillars, run from Afghanistan to Karnataka.

### Slide 1.4 (text): How he names himself
- On screen: devānaṃpiya piyadasi
- On screen: ‘Beloved of the Gods’, Piyadasi
> But in almost all of them, he does not use the name Ashoka.
> He calls himself Devanampiya Piyadasi. Devanampiya means Beloved of the Gods, and Piyadasi is a title.
> After James Prinsep deciphered the Brahmi script in 1837, scholars linked this Piyadasi with Ashoka, through the chronicles of Sri Lanka.
> The stones themselves, though, kept the name back.

### Slide 1.5 (text): Maski, January 1915
- On screen: devānaṃpiyasa asokasa
- On screen: ‘of the Beloved of the Gods, Asoka’
> That changed in January 1915. A mining engineer named Beadon found a minor rock edict at Maski, in today's Raichur district of Karnataka.
> It begins: Devanampiyasa Asokasa. Of the Beloved of the Gods, Asoka.

### Slide 1.6 (text): Only a handful use the name
- On screen: Maski
- On screen: Gujarra (Madhya Pradesh)
- On screen: Nittur and Udegolam (Karnataka)
> Only a handful of Ashoka's own inscriptions use his name at all.
> They are versions of his minor rock edicts, at Maski, at Gujarra in Madhya Pradesh, and at Nittur and Udegolam in Karnataka.

### Slide 1.7 (text): And no face
- On screen: Mauryan coins: punch-marked symbols, no royal portraits
- On screen: No statue or painting from his lifetime survives
> His face is missing too.
> Mauryan coins are punch-marked with symbols, not royal portraits.
> And no statue or painting made in his lifetime survives.
> So whatever we picture when we say Ashoka comes from later hands.

### Slide 1.8 (chapter): A collapsed roof in Sannati, 1986
> Chapter two. A collapsed roof in Sannati.

### Slide 1.9 (text): Sannati, on the Bhima river
- On screen: Kalikamba (Kali) shrine, Chandralamba temple complex
- On screen: Kalaburagi (Gulbarga) district, Karnataka
- On screen: 1986: the roof collapses
> The trail to the portrait begins in a temple.
> In 1986, the roof of the Kalikamba shrine, a shrine of the goddess Kali, in the Chandralamba temple complex at Sannati, collapsed and damaged the image.
> Sannati stands on the Bhima river, in Kalaburagi district of Karnataka, also known as Gulbarga.

### Slide 1.10 (text): The pedestal was an edict
- On screen: The stone under the image carried Ashokan edicts
- On screen: Unread, for a very long time
> When the image was moved, the stone pedestal on which it had stood turned out to carry portions of Ashoka's edicts.
> It had served as a pedestal, unread, for a very long time.
> That find turned attention to the surroundings. Nearby, at the village of Kanaganahalli, lay a low mound beside the river.

### Slide 1.11 (chapter): The great stupa by the Bhima
> Chapter three. The great stupa by the Bhima.

### Slide 1.12 (text): Kanaganahalli, from 1994
- On screen: Archaeological Survey of India, under K. P. Poonacha
- On screen: A Buddhist stupa, cased in carved limestone slabs
- On screen: Report: Memoirs of the ASI no. 106 (imprint 2011)
> The Archaeological Survey of India began excavating the Kanaganahalli mound in 1994, under K. P. Poonacha.
> The mound was a Buddhist stupa, a domed monument, cased in carved limestone slabs.
> The excavation report was published as Memoirs of the Archaeological Survey of India, number 106.

### Slide 1.13 (text): The panel
- On screen: A royal couple at the centre
- On screen: Turban, large earrings, a parasol held over the king
- On screen: Attendants with the parasol and fly-whisks (chāmara)
> Among the slabs is a damaged panel, showing a royal couple at the centre.
> The king wears a turban and large earrings. A parasol, the old sign of sovereignty, is held over him.
> His queen is beside him, and attendants hold the parasol and fly-whisks, called chamaras.

### Slide 1.14 (label): label
> Along the border above runs a single line in Brahmi. It is usually printed Raaya Asoko. King Ashoka.
> The scholarly edition of the Kanaganahalli inscriptions reads it Raayaa Asoko, with a long aa in the second sign.

### Slide 1.15 (text): The only ones that name him
- On screen: Sanchi and Amaravati: panels often identified as Ashoka, none named
- On screen: Kanaganahalli: a second slab carries a similar label
- On screen: So far, the only known carvings of Ashoka that name him
> Panels at Sanchi and Amaravati are often identified as Ashoka, and with good reason. But none of them carries his name.
> At Kanaganahalli, the sculptor removed the doubt. And a second slab on the same stupa carries a similar label.
> So far, these are the only known carvings of the emperor with an inscription naming him.

### Slide 1.16 (text): So far
- On screen: Ashoka rarely named himself; no lifetime likeness survives
- On screen: 1986: an edict found under a temple image at Sannati
- On screen: From 1994: the Kanaganahalli stupa, and a king labelled ‘King Asoka’
> Let us pause and take stock.
> Ashoka rarely used his own name, and no likeness of him from his lifetime survives.
> In 1986, an Ashokan edict turned up under a temple image at Sannati.
> And from 1994, the excavation of the stupa at Kanaganahalli revealed a carved king, labelled King Asoka.

### Slide 2.3 (text): When was it carved?
- On screen: Nayanjot Lahiri: 2nd century CE
- On screen: Scholars generally: early centuries CE
- On screen: Some three to four centuries after Ashoka’s death
> The panel is not a likeness.
> The historian Nayanjot Lahiri dates it to the second century CE. Scholars generally place the Kanaganahalli reliefs in the early centuries CE.
> That is some three to four centuries after Ashoka's death.

### Slide 2.4 (text): What they had was memory
- On screen: Buddhist traditions: Ashoka as the model Buddhist king
- On screen: Later gathered in texts such as the Aśokāvadāna
- On screen: Sātavāhana-period donors set him among the kings
> What the carvers had was memory.
> Buddhist traditions, later gathered in texts such as the Ashokavadana, remembered Ashoka as the model Buddhist king.
> And donors of the Satavahana period set him among the kings shown on their great stupa.

### Slide 2.5 (text): A stupa full of writing
- On screen: Well over a hundred short Brāhmī inscriptions
- On screen: Many of them name donors
- On screen: Enlarged and embellished under Sātavāhana rule
> The site yielded well over a hundred short inscriptions in Brahmi script. Many of them name donors.
> And the stupa was enlarged and embellished under the rule of the Satavahanas.

### Slide 2.6 (text): How we know
- On screen: K. P. Poonacha: the ASI excavation report (2011)
- On screen: Nakanishi and von Hinüber (2014): the inscriptions
- On screen: Monika Zin (2018): the 60 massive slabs covering the dome
> How do we know all this? From careful work on the stones, published in full.
> K. P. Poonacha's excavation report came out in the Memoirs of the Archaeological Survey of India.
> Maiko Nakanishi and Oskar von Hinuber edited the inscriptions in 2014, and the DHARMA project now publishes digital editions of them.
> And Monika Zin's 2018 book analyses the sixty massive slabs that once covered the dome.

### Slide 2.7 (text): Why it matters
- On screen: It does not show what Ashoka looked like
- On screen: It shows how he was remembered
- On screen: In the same region as his own edicts at Maski and Sannati
> That is what makes the panel valuable.
> It does not tell us what Ashoka looked like. It tells us how he was remembered.
> And that this memory was alive in northern Karnataka, the same region where his own edicts, at Maski, Sannati and elsewhere, had been cut into stone centuries earlier.

### Slide 2.8 (chapter): Still debated
> Chapter five. What is still debated.

### Slide 2.9 (text): Open questions
- On screen: The date: Lahiri’s 2nd century CE, or a broader early-centuries-CE range
- On screen: The label: rāya in the press, rāyā in the scholarly edition, rānyo in some exam guides
- On screen: So epigraphists go back to the stone, or a good photograph
> Two things are worth holding loosely.
> First, the date. Lahiri's second century CE is one scholar's view, inside a broader range of the early centuries CE.
> Second, the label itself. Books do not agree on how to print it. Newspapers print Raaya. The scholarly edition reads Raayaa. Some textbooks and exam guides print Raanyo.
> That is exactly why epigraphists, the people who read inscriptions, go back to the stone, or to a good photograph of it, before they quote a single letter.

### Slide 2.10 (chapter): Try it yourself
> Chapter six. Try it yourself.

### Slide 2.11 (brahmi): brahmi
> Brahmi is read from left to right. Each consonant sign carries a built-in short a.
> To give it another vowel, the scribe adds a small stroke to the consonant. A vowel at the start of a word has a letter of its own.
> So the label is written with five signs. Here they are as the scholarly edition reads them.
> Raa: r, with the long aa stroke. Yaa: y, with the long aa stroke again. A: the letter for an initial a. So: s, with the o stroke. And ko: k, with the o stroke.
> If the second sign had no stroke, it would read plain ya. That one small stroke is the whole difference between the two printed versions.
> Raaya, or raayaa, is a Prakrit word for king, related to Sanskrit raajan. Asoko is the Prakrit form of Ashoka.

- On screen: His name in his own inscriptions: Maski, Gujarra, Nittur, Udegolam

> What does the label mean? King Ashoka.
